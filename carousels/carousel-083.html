The Core Problem
Why most proposals lose before
the prospect reads page 3.
The average proposal is a document about the agency — not the client.

It features: company history, services list, team bios, case studies from unrelated industries, and a pricing table. The prospect reads page 1, skips to pricing, and makes their decision. The proposal is rarely the deciding factor — the relationship and trust built before it is.

The proposal's job: confirm and document what the client already feels about working with you.

The test: Count the words 'we' and 'our' vs. 'you' and 'your' on page 1. If 'we' wins, rewrite it. The client doesn't care about you — they care about their problem.

Winning Structure
The 5-page proposal structure
that wins deals.
The rule: 'you' and 'your' must dominate page 1.

Page 1: Your diagnosis of their specific situation (prove you listened). Page 2: The specific outcomes you'll deliver — not the tasks, the results. Page 3: Your approach and methodology (the how). Page 4: Timeline and investment. Page 5: Relevant case study matching their problem.

Every page answers a question the client is already asking. Page 1: Do they understand me? Page 2: What will I get? Page 4: What will it cost?

One-Page Proposal
The one-page proposal that converts
better than 10-page documents.
Simplicity signals confidence. Complexity signals insecurity.

For smaller engagements: one page, three sections. Section 1: 'Here's what I understand about your situation.' Section 2: 'Here's what I'd do and what you'll have at the end.' Section 3: 'Investment: £X. Next step: [link].'

A one-page proposal signals that you've thought clearly about the problem and the solution. It respects the client's time. Busy decision-makers prefer brevity.

When to use it: Engagements under £5,000. Clients who prefer direct communication. When the relationship is strong. When your reputation does the heavy lifting.

Common Mistakes
Proposal mistakes that lose deals
before you know it.
Fix these six things and your close rate improves immediately.

Generic language ('we deliver results'). Features without benefits. No specific mention of the prospect's situation. Sending without a verbal walkthrough. Too many options (decision paralysis). No expiry date — proposals with no urgency get indefinitely deferred.

The most common mistake: a proposal that could be sent to any client. If your prospect can't tell it was written specifically for them, rewrite it.

The Walkthrough
Why proposals need a meeting —
never just email it.
This single change doubles close rates.

Always schedule a 20-minute walkthrough call. 'I'd like to walk you through the proposal so I can answer any questions and make sure it addresses everything we discussed.'

On the walkthrough, you control the narrative. You emphasise what matters. You handle concerns in real time. You can watch for hesitation and address it immediately. An emailed proposal without a walkthrough is a 50% close-rate surrender.

The sequence: Discovery call → 24-hour follow-up summary → Proposal sent → Walkthrough call scheduled → Close. Never skip the walkthrough.

Proposal Template Structure

The 5-page proposal — what goes on each page

01
Their Situation
Your specific diagnosis of their problem. More 'you/your' than any other word. Prove you listened on the discovery call.
02
Outcomes
What the client will have at the end — not tasks, results. Specific, measurable, and tied to what they told you they want.
03
Methodology
How you'll achieve the outcomes. Your process, approach, and why it works. Confidence without over-complicating.
04
Timeline & Investment
Clear milestones, deliverable schedule, and investment. Include an expiry date to create healthy urgency.
05
Relevant Case Study
One case study matching their specific problem. Before/after. Real numbers where possible. Proof you've done this before.
The proposal doesn't close deals. The relationship, the discovery call, and the walkthrough close deals. The proposal documents what's already been decided.
