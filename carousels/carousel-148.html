The Delegation Principle
Delegate anything that doesn't
require your specific judgment,
voice, or relationships.
Keep everything that does. This is the only rule you need.

The single question that guides every delegation decision: Does this task require my unique judgment, voice, or relationship to be done well? If the answer is no — delegate it. If yes — keep it.

This question cuts through the anxiety most creators feel about outsourcing. It is not about whether someone else can do it adequately. It is about whether the output requires your specific presence to have the value your audience expects. Scheduling a post requires no unique judgment. Writing the post does. Designing a graphic requires skill, not your unique perspective. Deciding what the graphic should say requires your specific expertise.

The goal of delegation is to free time for the things only you can do — and do more of them.

Signs You're Ready to Hire
3 conditions that signal
it's time to get support.
Don't hire before the conditions exist — or after they've cost you growth.

Condition 1: Content ROI is proven. You know your content generates value — leads, revenue, or opportunities. You have something worth amplifying. Hiring before this means supporting a content strategy that hasn't been validated yet.

Condition 2: Specific time-draining tasks identified. You can name, specifically, what is eating your time that doesn't require your unique input. "I spend 3 hours a week scheduling and formatting posts" is ready to delegate. "I feel busy" is not.

Condition 3: Budget exists for 2+ months. Testing for one month and stopping due to cost is worse than not starting. Commit to 2 months minimum. If you cannot sustain that budget, it's too soon.

What to Delegate First
Five tasks that steal time
without requiring your voice.
These are the highest-value delegation decisions for most creators.

1. Scheduling and distribution. Formatting and scheduling your written posts across platforms. Pure execution, no judgment needed.

2. Graphic and carousel creation. You provide the content and structure; a designer translates it visually. Design systems make this highly delegatable once templates are established.

3. Research and sourcing. Finding statistics, case studies, and examples for your content. You synthesise and write; someone else gathers raw material.

4. Transcript editing. If you record audio or video and convert to text, editing the transcript is systematic, not creative.

5. Comment management. Monitoring and flagging comments that need your response. You reply; someone else surfaces the ones worth replying to.

What to Never Delegate
Three things that must
always stay with you.
These are the irreducible core of a personal brand.

The ideas. Even if someone else formats or frames them, the original thinking must come from you. Ghost-written content built on someone else's ideas produces an audience that trusts a persona, not a person. When the gap is eventually discovered, the trust collapses.

Final approval on all published content. Everything that publishes under your name should pass through your judgment before it goes live. A single off-brand post can undo months of brand-building. Non-negotiable.

Strategic positioning. What you stand for, what problems you solve, what offers you promote, how you want to be perceived. These decisions belong entirely to you. No one else can make them correctly on your behalf — and getting them wrong is expensive to repair.

Finding Your First Hire
Start with 10 hours/month.
Paid test project first.
Expand hours as trust builds. Don't overcommit early.

The most common first hire for a LinkedIn creator is a part-time virtual assistant at 10 hours per month. This is enough to handle scheduling, light research, and formatting — without overcommitting to costs before you know the working relationship works.

Finding the right person: LinkedIn is actually one of the best places to find content-aware VAs (search "content VA" or "social media VA"). Ask for referrals from other creators. Use platforms like Contra or Worksome for freelancers.

Always start with a paid test project before committing to an ongoing arrangement. Give a clearly scoped, one-off task. Evaluate the output and the communication. Only if both are strong do you move to an ongoing relationship. Expand hours by 50–100% as trust and performance accumulate.

Test project scope: "Format and schedule 4 posts this week, following this template." Clear deliverable, clear timeline, low risk. Results speak for themselves.

Delegation Framework

Task Keep or Delegate? Why
Original idea generation Keep forever Requires your unique perspective and experience
Writing the post draft Keep forever Your voice is the product
Final approval before publish Keep forever Brand risk too high to skip
Formatting + scheduling Delegate first Pure execution, no judgment needed
Carousel / graphic design Delegate when ready Skills-based, templatable, repeatable
Research + data sourcing Delegate when ready Systematic, not creative
Comment surfacing / filtering Delegate when volume demands You still reply; they flag what needs response
Hire to free time for the irreplaceable work. More creation time always outperforms more production capacity.
