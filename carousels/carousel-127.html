Why Storytelling Works
Stories bypass skepticism
that facts trigger.
The neuroscience behind why stories outperform data.

When you read a statistic, your prefrontal cortex evaluates it critically. When you read a story, your brain simulates the experience — you feel what the protagonist feels. Narrative processing bypasses skepticism.

Stories are shared 22× more than facts alone. They make abstract expertise concrete — instead of "I'm a good strategist", a story shows a specific client outcome that proves it without claiming it.

Data tells people what to think. Stories make them feel it themselves. Feeling drives action.

The 5-Part Structure
Every great LinkedIn story
uses this skeleton.
Simple structure. Infinite stories.

1. Situation: Set the scene in one sentence. Who, where, what context.
2. Complication: What went wrong, changed, or created pressure. This is the hook.
3. Your Insight: The moment of realisation or the choice you made differently.
4. Resolution: What happened as a result. Specific outcome, not vague success.
5. Lesson: The transferable truth that applies to your reader's situation — not your story.

The test: If someone can read your lesson without reading your story and it still lands — your story is doing its job as context, not content.

Finding Stories
Stories are hiding in your
everyday work.
You don't need drama. You need attention.

Most professionals think they don't have stories. They're wrong — they have stories every day. They just don't recognise them as content material.

Look for: client conversations that shifted something. Project post-mortems — what you'd do differently. The moment confusion became clarity on a problem you'd been stuck on. An old belief you held that you now think is wrong.

The best stories aren't dramatic. They're specific. "A SaaS founder, 3 years in, told me something that changed how I think about pricing" works better than "clients often ask me about pricing."

Making Stories Relatable
Specificity is what makes
stories universal.
The counter-intuitive truth about audience connection.

Vague stories feel safe to write but don't land. Specific stories feel exposed but connect deeply. Use specificity: "a SaaS founder 3 years in" not "a client."

Show tension before insight — the harder the problem sounds, the more valuable the solution feels. End with a truth that lands in someone else's situation, not yours.

People don't see themselves in your broad experience. They see themselves in your specific detail — because specificity triggers the "that's exactly me" recognition that makes them comment.

Specificity test: Replace every vague noun with a specific one. "Someone I know" → "a founder I spoke to last Tuesday." Feels uncomfortable. That's right.

Hook Mastery
Your first sentence decides
if anyone reads the rest.
The opening line is where most story posts are won or lost.

LinkedIn shows 2-3 lines before "see more." Your first sentence — sometimes your only sentence — must create enough tension or curiosity to earn the click.

Can you end it with a comma and still stop the scroll? That's the test. "Three years ago I made a mistake that cost me my biggest client," earns the click. "I want to share something about client management" does not.

Great story hooks are incomplete without the rest. They create an open loop — the brain physically needs it closed. Give people an open loop. They will follow you in.

Story Framework Template

5-part structure with prompts and examples

Part What It Does Writing Prompt
Situation Sets context in one sentence "I was [doing X] when [specific detail]..."
Complication Creates tension — the hook "Then [unexpected thing happened]..."
Insight Your unique perspective shift "That's when I realised [non-obvious truth]..."
Resolution Specific outcome, not vague success "The result was [concrete, named outcome]..."
Lesson Transferable truth for reader "If you're [reader situation], then [lesson]..."
The lesson is what your reader will share. Write the story for yourself. Write the lesson for them.
