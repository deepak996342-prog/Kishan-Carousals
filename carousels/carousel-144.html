What Expert Licensing Means
Other businesses pay to use
your frameworks. You get paid.
Create once. Get paid repeatedly. No delivery required.

Expert licensing is the model where other businesses pay you to use your intellectual property — your methodology, your frameworks, your content, your curriculum — in their own products, services, or training programs. You create the IP once. They deploy it. You receive a licensing fee.

This is fundamentally different from consulting or coaching, where your time is the product. In licensing, your thinking is the product. The same framework can be licensed to 10 different businesses simultaneously. Your income scales without additional time. Most experts sitting on a proven methodology have no idea this revenue model is available to them — or how straightforward the first deal can be.

The licensing test: Do you have a documented methodology, framework, or curriculum that has produced consistent results? If yes — you have licensable IP. The question is whether you've chosen to monetize it.

Licensing Models
Three ways to license
your expertise to others.
Each model has a different delivery requirement and revenue structure.

Training licensing: Your course or curriculum is taught by another organisation's trainers. You receive a per-seat fee or annual licence. The organisation gains ready-built training content; you gain passive income from their delivery infrastructure.

Methodology licensing: Another consultancy uses your named framework as part of their client delivery. They train their team on your methodology and pay a licence fee to use your IP brand. Common in management consulting and L&D.

Content licensing: Your written content, tools, templates, or assessments are embedded in another business's product or platform. Clean, scalable, low-maintenance. Each model produces income without ongoing delivery from you.

Who Buys Expert Licences
Five buyer types that
regularly licence expert IP.
Knowing who to approach makes your first deal far more achievable.

The buyers of expert licensing arrangements are typically organisations that need your content but either lack the time to create it or the authority to carry the methodology credibly. Training companies (they need curriculum content and will pay for quality), management consultancies and agencies (they want to embed your methodology in their service offering), publishers (books, e-learning platforms, content libraries), educational institutions (universities, business schools seeking practitioner expertise), and corporate L&D departments (large companies building internal training programmes).

These buyers have budgets for this. The challenge is not demand — it is positioning yourself clearly enough that they can see you as a licensing candidate.

Positioning for Licensing
Three things that make
your IP licensable.
Authority, documentation, and proven results signal a licensable asset.

A named, documented methodology. Unnamed processes cannot be licensed. Give your framework a name. Document it in enough detail that someone else could follow it. Your LinkedIn content is the publication platform — sharing your methodology publicly builds the proof that it works and the IP trail that establishes authorship.

LinkedIn authority in your domain. Licensing buyers do due diligence. They search your name. They read your content. A 12–18 month LinkedIn content history demonstrating consistent expertise in a specific area is the strongest possible portfolio.

Case studies showing the framework works. Results > credentials. Two or three case studies where your methodology produced measurable outcomes is the most persuasive licensing document you can create.

Starting Your First Deal
Identify 3 targets. Build the deck.
Book a 30-minute call.
The first licensing deal is simpler than most experts believe.

Most experts overcomplicate the licensing process before making any approach. The first deal requires three things: a target list (identify 3 organisations that serve your target audience and would benefit from your methodology — training companies, L&D departments, agencies), a simple licensing deck (10 slides: your methodology overview, the problem it solves, 2–3 case studies, commercial terms, and what licensees receive), and a 30-minute pitch call (walk through the deck, focus on their outcomes).

Commercial terms for a first deal: Annual licence fee (£5,000–£25,000 depending on scope), per-seat royalty, or revenue share. Negotiate what you're comfortable with, but start higher than you think is reasonable.

First deal shortcut: Offer a pilot licence at a reduced rate for 6 months. Lower barrier for them; you gain a case study and a renewal at full rate.
