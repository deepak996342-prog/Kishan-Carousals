Common Mistakes
Why discovery calls fail —
the 3 most common mistakes.
A discovery call should feel like a consultation, not a sales meeting.

Mistake 1: Pitching immediately without understanding the prospect's situation. Mistake 2: Talking more than listening. Mistake 3: Trying to close on the first call.

The prospect should feel heard, understood, and clearer after the call — regardless of whether they buy. Your job on a discovery call is to understand, not to convince.

The benchmark: You should be speaking 30% of the time and listening 70%. If you're speaking more, you're pitching. Stop pitching. Start asking.

The SPIN Framework
The question sequence that
creates internal motivation to buy.
You didn't create the motivation — you uncovered it.

Situation: 'What does your current content process look like?' Problem: 'What's not working about that?' Implication: 'What does that cost you — in time, clients, revenue?' Need-payoff: 'What would change in your business if that was solved?'

This sequence creates internal motivation to buy. Each question builds on the last. By the need-payoff question, the prospect has articulated their own case for your service.

5-Question Structure
The 5-question discovery
call structure.
Ask, then listen. Deeply.

1. 'Tell me about your business and where you're at right now.' 2. 'What made you reach out today? What's the specific problem you're looking to solve?' 3. 'What have you tried before to solve this?' 4. 'What does success look like in 90 days if we work together?' 5. 'What would it cost you to not solve this in the next 6 months?'

Five questions. Every answer tells you exactly what to put in your proposal.

The Follow-Up
The follow-up email that closes
what the call started.
Send within 2 hours. This email does more selling than most sales pitches.

The formula: 'Based on what you shared, here's what I think is happening [specific diagnosis], here's what I'd do [your approach], here's what it would take [scope + investment], next step [booking link].'

This email works because it's personalised to their specific situation — not a generic proposal template. It proves you listened. It demonstrates competence before you're hired.

Handling Objections
How to handle
'I need to think about it.'
This phrase usually means: unclear ROI, unresolved concern, or wrong person on the call.

Ask: 'Absolutely, what specifically do you need to think through? I want to make sure you have everything you need to make a decision.'

The answer gives you what to address. Never leave 'I need to think about it' without finding out what's underneath it. Every unspoken concern is a lost deal. Every surfaced concern is a solvable problem.

The rule: 'I need to think about it' is not a no. It's an invitation to ask a better question. Dig one level deeper and you'll find the real answer.
