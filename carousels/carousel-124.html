The Core Distinction
Evergreen vs. Timely:
Why It Matters
Both have a place. Only one compounds.

Most LinkedIn content falls into one of two categories, with very different shelf lives:

Timely content — news commentary, trend reactions, current events, quarterly reports. Shelf life: 24–48 hours. Timely content gets a burst of reach when it's fresh. Then it's functionally dead. Nobody searches for "my reaction to the March 2024 algorithm update" in September.

Evergreen content — frameworks, psychological principles, fundamentals of your field, personal transformation stories, "how I think about X" insights. Shelf life: 12+ months, often years.

Evergreen content can be shared with a brand-new audience member in 2026 and be as relevant as the day it was written. It doesn't reference dates, news, or time-bound context. It speaks to permanent human motivations, persistent professional challenges, and enduring principles.

5 Archetypes
5 Evergreen Content
Archetypes That Work
These five formats reliably produce long-shelf-life, high-engagement content.

1. Framework posts. "The 3-step process I use to [outcome]." A named, repeatable system with clear stages. Saves are high because people want to reference it later.

2. Myth-busting posts. "Everyone says X. Here's why X is wrong." Challenges a widely-held belief with evidence or alternative framing. Timeless because the myth persists.

3. Step-by-step guides. Practical, sequential, outcome-specific. "How to do X in Y steps" never expires as long as the process remains valid.

4. Personal transformation stories. Your experience going from A to B. The emotional truth of a transformation story doesn't age — because the struggle you describe is ongoing for people who find it years later.

5. "This changed how I think about X" insights. A single idea that fundamentally shifted your perspective. Written as a revelation, not a listicle.

Writing for Permanence
Writing Content That
Stays Relevant for Years
Four rules that guarantee a post can be reshared without edits in 2 years.

The difference between a post that ages well and one that doesn't often comes down to four specific writing choices:

No dates. "Last week I tried..." instantly dates the post. "I once tried..." doesn't. Remove all date references that aren't essential to the point.

No "last week" or "recently." Same reason. Time-anchored language creates expiry dates. Write in the eternal present tense of principles.

No news references. "With the current state of AI..." is dated within months. "As AI capabilities evolve..." stays relevant indefinitely.

Principle-based, not trend-based. Anchor the point in a psychological principle, a business fundamental, or a human truth rather than a current trend. The principle will still be true when the trend is forgotten.

Specific enough to be useful in 2 years. Vague content isn't evergreen — it's just forgettable. Specificity and permanence are not opposites.

The Resharing Strategy
The Resharing
Strategy
Your best post from 6 months ago is new content for 80% of your current audience.

LinkedIn audiences grow and turn over continuously. If you gained 500 followers in the last 3 months, those 500 people have never seen your best post from a year ago. It's not old to them — it doesn't exist yet.

Reshare evergreen posts every 3–4 months. This is not laziness. It's strategic distribution. The same post, reaching a meaningfully different audience segment, produces similar results to when it was first published.

Add a new first line to freshen it. A single updated line — "I published this a year ago. It's still the most accurate thing I've written about X." — signals that you're sharing intentionally, not accidentally recycling old content. It also creates a brief moment of meta-commentary that often performs well in its own right.

A library of 30 evergreen posts on rotation means 8+ posts per month require zero new writing.

Building the Library
Building Your
Evergreen Library
30 posts. 4-month rotation. Index by theme. Done.

The evergreen library is a finite, manageable asset. You don't need 200 posts — you need 30 great ones.

Target: 30 evergreen posts. Six per archetype (frameworks, myth-busting, guides, transformation stories, perspective-changing insights). A set of 30 covers every major theme in your content pillars and provides material for an 18-month rotation with no repetition.

4-month rotation cycle. Each post is reshared once every 4 months. With 30 posts, you're publishing ~8 evergreen reshares per month — roughly 2 per week. Combined with new content, this creates a consistent, high-quality feed with significantly less writing effort.

Index by theme in Notion. A simple database: post title, core theme, archetype, last shared date, next scheduled share date, performance notes. Takes 30 minutes to build. Saves hours every month.

New content to pair with: 2–3 original posts/week

Build the library one post per week. In 30 weeks you have a permanent, rotating content asset that works for you indefinitely.
