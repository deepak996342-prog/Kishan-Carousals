What Counts as a Digital Product
You already have the
raw material. Package it.
Digital products are packaged expertise — not software, not complexity.

A digital product is any piece of knowledge, process, or tool you can package once and deliver digitally without custom work each time. The format options available to a LinkedIn creator include: Notion templates, email swipe files, prompt libraries for AI tools, mini-courses, frameworks as PDFs, step-by-step playbooks, curated resource guides, and SOPs for a specific task.

The common thread is not the format — it's the transformation. Every digital product should answer: what does the buyer achieve or avoid by purchasing this? If your answer is vague, your product will undersell. Make the transformation specific: "Go from blank page to 30 posts in a weekend" beats "LinkedIn content templates".

Quick test: If buying your product saves someone 10 hours or helps them avoid a specific mistake, it's worth making. Price accordingly.

Finding Your Idea
The right idea is already
hiding in plain sight.
The intersection of demand, repetition, and time savings.

The best digital product ideas sit at the intersection of three signals: the most common requests you receive (demand is already proven), the things you repeatedly explain to clients or connections (you have the expertise and the content), and something that saves someone 10+ hours (justifies paying for it rather than figuring it out themselves).

Review your last 90 days: What have clients asked you to help with most? What LinkedIn comments ask the same question? What does your free advice most consistently cover? That territory contains your first product. The market has already voted — you just need to listen to the vote.

Idea validation shortcut: Post a LinkedIn poll asking your audience which of 3 potential product ideas they'd find most useful. The answer in 24 hours tells you everything.

The Weekend Build
Day 1: create it.
Day 2: sell it.
A weekend is enough time if you stop overthinking and start building.

Day 1: Outline the entire product in the morning. Identify every section, template, or piece of content it needs. Build in Canva (design-heavy), Notion (template or guide), or Google Docs (frameworks, playbooks). Don't refine — create. Aim for 80% complete by evening.

Day 2: Add examples, refine copy, and create your sales page on Gumroad or Lemon Squeezy (both take 30 minutes to set up). Write a compelling product description that leads with transformation. Set your price. Upload. Your product is live.

The goal isn't perfect — it's published. You will improve it based on buyer feedback. The first version is just the starting point.

LinkedIn as Sales Channel
Sell the transformation,
not the features.
3–4 teaser posts before launch warm the audience that's already ready.

The pre-launch content sequence matters. For 1–2 weeks before launch, publish 3–4 posts that share the problem your product solves, the transformation it creates, and a behind-the-scenes preview of what's inside. Never lead with "I've built a template pack." Lead with the pain: "I spent 6 hours this week building LinkedIn carousels. Then I templated the process. Here's what I built."

On launch day, post your announcement and personally DM your 20 warmest connections — people who engage with your content regularly. A personal message from you converts far better than any public post. Early buyers create social proof that accelerates the next wave of sales.

Pre-launch checklist: 3 teaser posts published · Sales page live · DM list of 20 warm connections ready · Launch post drafted with strong hook and clear link

Pricing Your Digital Product
Lower prices signal
lower value. Price up.
The buyer's psychology is counterintuitive: higher price = higher trust.

The most common mistake first-time digital product creators make is under-pricing. They charge £5–£10 because they fear rejection and don't believe in their own expertise. A well-designed, transformation-focused digital product priced at £25–£97 is not only normal — it's expected. Pricing below £20 signals "low effort, low value" to your audience. Pricing at £47–£97 signals "this is professional and worthwhile."

The buyer isn't comparing your product to free content — they're comparing it to the cost of the problem. If your template saves someone 6 hours, and their time is worth £50/hour, your product is worth £300 just in time saved. Price to the value delivered, not the cost of production.

Pricing rule: Start at £47. If it sells well, test £67 or £97 with the next launch. Your price is not final — it's a starting hypothesis.
