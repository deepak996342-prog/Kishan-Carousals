When to Hire
The Right Time
to Hire Content Help
Hiring before these conditions are met usually makes things worse, not better.

Hiring too early is as damaging as hiring too late. Before you bring anyone in, four conditions need to be true:

Content ROI is proven. You have evidence — in DMs, calls, or revenue — that your content is generating business results. You're not hiring to fix a content problem; you're hiring to scale a content success.

You're at capacity. You're doing as much content work as you personally can and the bottleneck is genuinely hours, not ideas or strategy.

Time is genuinely the constraint. Not perfectionism. Not fear of delegating your voice. Actual time that cannot be recaptured without help.

Budget for 2+ months exists. Never hire for one month of work. The ramp-up time means you won't see returns until month 2 at the earliest. Plan accordingly.

What to Delegate
What to Delegate
First
Start with the tasks that consume time without requiring your voice or judgment.

The first hire should never be a ghostwriter. Your voice is your differentiator. Delegate process, not personality.

Scheduling and publishing. Loading drafted posts into Buffer, setting publish times, managing the content calendar. Pure process. Zero judgment needed.

Carousel and graphic creation. You provide the content and structure; they produce the visual execution. Canva templates make this highly systematisable.

Research. Background research on topics, data points, competitor analysis, industry news summaries. You get the raw material; you do the thinking.

Transcript editing. If you record video or audio, they turn the raw transcript into a clean, structured document you can then write from.

Comment management. Monitoring your posts, flagging replies that need your personal response, thanking new commenters. High time cost, low creativity requirement.

Where to Find Help
Where to Find
the Right Person
The source matters as much as the candidate.

Three channels, each suited to a different type of hire:

Upwork for task-based work. Best for defined, repeatable deliverables — graphic creation, scheduling, research. Hourly or per-project. Low risk. Easy to start and stop. Filter by reviews and portfolio, not just price.

LinkedIn for specialists. If you need someone with genuine content strategy or writing expertise, LinkedIn search finds professionals with a track record. They cost more. They're worth it for creative roles.

Your own audience for culture-fit hires. The most underutilised channel. People who follow you already understand your voice, your values, and your worldview. A post saying "I'm looking for a content assistant — here's what the role involves" will surface people who genuinely want to work with you specifically.

The best first hire is often someone already in your community — a follower who comments consistently, understands your work, and responds when you post the opportunity.

The Hiring Test
The Paid Mini-Project
Hiring Test
Never commit to anyone you haven't seen work in a real, paid context.

Interviews reveal almost nothing about work quality, especially in creative and content roles. The hiring test reveals everything.

Assign a paid mini-project of 2–3 hours' work. Real work. Something you actually need done. Pay them fairly for the time — this is not an audition at the candidate's expense, it's a paid evaluation.

The mini-project reveals four things that CVs and interviews can't:

Initiative. Do they ask clarifying questions before starting, or just deliver something generic?
Quality. Does the output meet your standard, or require extensive rework?
Communication. Do they update you as the project progresses? Do they flag problems early?
Turnaround. Do they meet the agreed timeline, or do you have to chase?

Two candidates pass the interview. Only one passes the mini-project. That's your hire.

Managing Well
Managing a Content
Collaborator Effectively
Clear systems prevent the most common collaboration failures.

Most content collaboration fails not because the person was wrong but because the management system was unclear.

Weekly 30-minute brief. Every Monday: what's being published this week, any brand updates, any feedback from last week. 30 minutes of alignment prevents hours of rework.

Shared Notion workspace. A single source of truth for the content calendar, brand guidelines, editorial standards, and in-progress drafts. Both of you work from the same document set.

Monthly feedback call. A structured 45-minute session reviewing what's working, what isn't, and what the next month's priorities are. Not a critique session — a collaborative planning session.

Clear editorial guidelines. A written document covering: your brand voice adjectives, topics that are on/off brand, formatting preferences, tone examples, and sample posts that represent the standard.
